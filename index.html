Division I • Medical Devices

Medical Device Distribution

For manufacturers who want to sell in Asia without building a regional organisation first. We aim to handle the route from Singapore registration to delivery, across the full range of device classes.

Step one

Product-Solution Fit & TAM / SAM Alignment

Clinical Need vs. Economic Desire

  • •Validate procedural pain points: does the device mitigate complications, reduce clinical length of stay, or eliminate blind insertion errors (e.g., sensor-guided NG tube verification)?
  • •Map the economic buyer: identify whether procurement decisions rest with hospital value committees (cost-per-bedday reduction) or private clinics (throughput and out-of-pocket margin).

Quantified Market Sizing

  • •TAM (Total Addressable Market): Overall regional capital and consumable spend within the therapeutic category across South Asia and APAC.
  • •SAM (Serviceable Addressable Market): Addressable procedural volumes across targeted care tiers—Tertiary Public Hospitals, Private Specialist Centers, Ambulatory GPs, Nursing Homes, and Veterinary Practices.
  • •SOM (Serviceable Obtainable Market): 3-year commercial capture target aligned with your sales force capacity and tender cycles.

Trajectory & Margin Viability

  • •Determine product lifecycle dynamics: recurring single-use consumable versus capital hardware with recurring service/kit attachments.
Step two

Multi-Territory Regulatory Clearances & Timelines

Anchor Clearance

Months 0–3
  • •Singapore (HSA): Establish local GDPMDS (SS 620) compliance (2–3 months). Clear Class A via notification (immediate to 10 days); submit Class B (1–3 months) and Class C (3–6 months) via abridged routes.

Abridged Regional Routes via Reference Recognition

Months 3–6
  • •Malaysia (MDA): CAB verification and MDA abridged review (2–4 months for Class B; 4–7 months for Class C).
  • •Thailand (Thai FDA): CSDT submission via reliance pathway (Class 2: 2–4 months; Class 3: 4–7 months).
  • •Vietnam (MoH): Fast-track Decree 98 registration leveraging HSA reference approval (Class A/B: 1–2 months; Class C: 3–6 months).

Direct National Technical Clearances

Months 4–8
  • •India (CDSCO): Form MD-14/MD-15 import evaluation via authorized agent (Class B: 3–5 months; Class C: 4–7 months).
  • •Indonesia (BPOM / MoH): Local IDAK/PAK holder review and CDAKB alignment (Class B: 3–5 months; Class C: 5–8 months).

Software Compliance Track

Parallel
  • •Complete cybersecurity (ISO 27001), patient data sovereignty checks, and Synapxe/NEHR interoperability verification for digital health/SaMD lines.
Step three

Go-To-Market Execution: Entry, Growth & Sustainability

Entry Phase

Months 0–12
  • •Secure clinical validation through 2–3 flagship tertiary academic centers.
  • •Deploy direct technical specialists in Singapore to establish standard-of-care protocols and overcome clinical objections before channel handoff.
  • •Use evaluation placements and reagent-rental agreements to bypass slow institutional capital budget approvals.

Growth Phase

Months 12–36
  • •Cascade adoption down from tertiary hospitals to community facilities, day surgery clinics, nursing homes, and veterinary networks.
  • •Appoint and manage audited in-country distribution partners in Malaysia, Thailand, Vietnam, Indonesia, and India using Singapore as the operational and training hub.
  • •Monitor and submit bids for Ministry of Health multi-year tender cycles 6 to 12 months in advance.

Sustainability Phase

Years 3–5+
  • •Razor-and-Blade Recurring Economics: Anchor long-term revenues on steady consumable reorders (e.g., tube placement sensors, specialized imaging kits) rather than one-off capital sales.
  • •Software Integration Moat: Pair physical hardware with custom SaMD, remote monitoring, and clinic management integrations, making replacement by commodity hardware competitors operationally prohibitive.
  • •Performance-Tied Exclusivity: Enforce minimum sales milestones in manufacturer distribution contracts to protect master territory rights across all operational markets.

Timelines are indicative and vary by product, device class and regulator. HFP is being set up now. Licences and registrations are being put in place alongside incorporation, and we will list them here once they are held.

How we take software to market

Step one

Product-Solution Fit & Market Sizing

Workflow Need vs. Economic Desire

  • •Pain point: does the software cut documentation time, reduce clinical or administrative errors, improve patient throughput, or shorten length of stay?
  • •Economic buyer: hospital IT and value committees weighing licence budgets and integration cost, or private clinics weighing subscription price and ease of onboarding.

Quantified Market Sizing

  • •TAM: overall regional health-IT and clinical-software spend in the category across South Asia and APAC.
  • •SAM: sites and seats across the care settings we can serve: tertiary hospitals, private specialist centres, GPs, nursing homes and veterinary practices.
  • •SOM: a 3-year capture target matched to our implementation capacity and public procurement cycles.

Model & Margin Viability

  • •Subscription or SaaS versus perpetual licence with maintenance, and what integration and support really cost to deliver.
Step two

Classification, Compliance & Interoperability

Classify Early

At the start
  • •Decide whether the software qualifies as a medical device (SaMD) in each market. Qualifying software follows a registration route; the rest does not.

Singapore as Anchor

  • •Health Sciences Authority route where the software qualifies, with the same technical documentation reused for reference-based routes in other regulators.

Security, Data & Interoperability Track

Parallel
  • •Information-security controls (ISO 27001), patient-data residency and privacy checks, and interoperability with national health-record systems such as Singapore’s NEHR through open standards like HL7 FHIR.

Localise for Each Market

  • •Language, data hosting, clinical terminology and local workflow adaptation before launch.
Step three

Go-to-Market & Development: Entry, Growth & Sustainability

Entry Phase

Year 1
  • •Secure proof-of-value pilots at a small number of flagship hospitals and clinics.
  • •Place implementation specialists in Singapore to set up workflows and overcome clinical and IT objections.
  • •Use time-limited pilots and subscription starts to avoid slow capital budget approvals.

Growth Phase

Years 1–3
  • •Cascade from tertiary hospitals to community facilities, day-surgery clinics, nursing homes and veterinary networks.
  • •Appoint and manage audited in-country resellers and implementation partners, using Singapore as the operational and training hub.
  • •Track public IT and health procurement cycles well ahead of bid dates.

Sustainability Phase

Year 3+
  • •Recurring revenue: anchor income on subscriptions, support and usage rather than one-off licences.
  • •Integration moat: pair software with distributed devices, remote monitoring and clinic-management integrations so it is hard to replace.
  • •Co-development: build custom modules and SaMD roadmaps with vendors and providers on a shared-risk basis.
  • •Performance-tied exclusivity: set minimum milestones in vendor distribution contracts to protect territory rights.
